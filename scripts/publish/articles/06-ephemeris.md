# Your Ephemeris, Explained: The Almanac No Astrologer Can Skip

An ephemeris is just a table: for each day, it lists the exact position of the Sun, Moon, planets and lunar nodes. Astronomers have compiled them for centuries, and every horoscope ever written traces its numbers back to one. Zunara's tools are built on the same VSOP87 theory the modern almanacs use.

**Why the position matters**

The first thing any competent chart reading asks is "where were the planets, precisely, at this moment?" A birth chart that's off by even a few minutes of arc can place a planet in a different degree — and for questions of houses and aspects, precision is the whole game. The astronomy is the load-bearing wall; the interpretation decorates it.

**How to read the table**

Sun and planets come as degrees within a sign, either by longitude or in zodiacal notation. The Moon moves fast — roughly thirteen degrees a day — so its row changes visibly hour to hour. When a planet shows 0 degrees in a sign followed by a direction marker, it's changing signs; that's a classical "ingress" moment that matters in predictive work.

**Why you should check rather than trust a headline**

Anyone can paste a horoscope into a feed. But you can verify the underlying data yourself in about ten seconds: open an ephemeris, find your birth date, read the Sun's position. When the tool and the table agree, you know you're looking at astronomy rather than storytelling. That one habit separates serious practice from astrology-as-comfort-blanket.
