# Eclipses, Explained: The Astronomy Behind the Fear

An eclipse is the Sun, Moon and Earth lining up so exactly that one body blocks the light we'd usually see from another. That's the whole mechanical story. Everything dramatic you read around it — world-ending predictions, "blood moons as signs" — is human storytelling layered on top of a repeatable geometry.

**The two kinds**

A solar eclipse happens when the Moon passes between the Sun and Earth, casting its shadow on us. A lunar eclipse happens when Earth gets between the Sun and the Moon, and our planet's shadow darkens the Moon's face — the orange-red "blood" color is just sunlight bending through Earth's atmosphere, the same reason a sunset looks red.

**Why they're rare as events**

The Moon's orbit is tilted about five degrees to Earth's orbit around the Sun, so the three bodies usually miss lining up perfectly. Eclipses only occur near the two points a year where the paths cross. That tilt — pure orbital mechanics — is why a total solar eclipse at any one spot on Earth is a once-or-twice-in-a-lifetime sight.
