# Mercury Retrograde, Explained Without the Fear-Mongering

*[Why planets appear to move backward — and why that's calmer than the internet says.]*

Every few months, Mercury "goes retrograde" and the internet fills with warnings about deleted text messages and missed flights. Let's actually look at what the physics says — because the reality is calmer, and honestly more interesting.

**What retrograde actually is**

Mercury doesn't slow down or reverse. It keeps orbiting the Sun at about 47 kilometers per second. Retrograde is an optical illusion from Earth's perspective: when our faster orbit overtakes Mercury (or any outer planet), it briefly appears to drift backward against the stars, like a car you're passing that seems to move backward relative to the road.

It's the same reason a planet's apparent motion oscillates. It's geometry, not mystery.

**Why it feels real to people**

Because something observable does change: the planet has shifted position in the sky, so astrologers read it from a different angle. In reflective astrology traditions, retrograde periods are associated with review, revisiting, and redoing — finishing old conversations rather than starting new ones. That's a useful idea, retrograde or not.

**The middle path**

You can hold both truths: the astronomy is exact (the planet's apparent backward loop is measurable to a fraction of a degree), and the meaning is interpretive (what you do with the symbolism is your choice). What you shouldn't do is blame a planet for a bad week.
